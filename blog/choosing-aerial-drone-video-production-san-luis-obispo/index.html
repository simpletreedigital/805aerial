How to Choose an Aerial Drone Video Production Company in San Luis Obispo County

What to check before you book, beyond how good the demo reel looks.

Published November 2026 · 805 Aerial · 8 min read

More real estate agents, wineries, and businesses across San Luis Obispo County are asking for aerial drone video as part of a standard marketing package. That demand has brought more operators into the market too, and the gap between a licensed, experienced aerial video production company and someone with a consumer drone and a Instagram page is often invisible until after a shoot goes wrong. A good demo reel tells you someone can edit. It doesn't tell you whether they can legally fly over the property you need, or whether they'll show up at all if the marine layer hasn't cleared.

Start With Licensing and Insurance, Not the Demo Reel

Any drone flight done for pay in the United States requires the pilot to hold an FAA Remote Pilot Certificate under Part 107. This isn't optional paperwork. It's the baseline that separates a commercial operator from a hobbyist, and it's the first thing to ask about before discussing price or availability. A company that gets vague when you ask for their certificate, or can't describe in their own words what Part 107 actually covers, is worth a second look before you book.

Ask about liability insurance too. A drone flying over a property, a crowd at an event, or a construction site carries real risk if something goes wrong mechanically or in the air. An operator who carries coverage and can speak to it plainly is signaling they treat this as a professional operation, not a side hustle.

Local note: San Luis Obispo County includes controlled airspace near San Luis Obispo County Regional Airport and Paso Robles Municipal Airport. An operator working here should be able to explain, without hesitation, how they check airspace before a flight rather than just showing up and launching.

Local Airspace and Terrain Knowledge Matters More Here

The Central Coast isn't a flat, easy market to fly. Coastal bluffs, dense hillside towns like Morro Bay, state park land, and protected habitat near some beaches all come with their own restrictions on where a drone can legally go. A flight plan built for a flat inland market often doesn't transfer directly to a shoot over the dunes above Pismo Beach or a hillside property in Cambria. Ask a prospective operator how they'd approach your specific location, not just whether they "fly drones." Someone who has actually worked the terrain here will have a specific answer, not a generic one.

Ask What Equipment and Editing Process They Actually Use

A consumer drone on auto-pilot mode produces noticeably different footage than a stabilized, cinema-grade platform flown with composed shots, using leading lines and elevation changes the way a cinematographer would compose a ground shot. Ask to see unedited or lightly edited footage, not just the polished final cut, to get a sense of what the raw material actually looks like before it's color graded and set to music.

Also ask about delivery. Depending on the project, you may need MLS-ready video for a real estate listing, vertical cuts for Instagram and TikTok, or full-resolution masters for your own archive. A production company should be able to tell you upfront what formats are included, not figure it out after the shoot.

Questions Worth Asking Before You Book

  • What does a typical discovery call cover? A production company worth hiring will want to understand your goal, location, and timeline before quoting a flight, and should flag any airspace or access concerns at that stage, not on shoot day.
  • How do you handle weather? Morning marine layer fog is common along the coast. Ask how flexible the operator is about rescheduling if conditions don't clear, and whether that flexibility is built into the quote.
  • What's the turnaround on a first cut, and how many revisions are included? Get this in writing, not just a verbal estimate.
  • Does this shoot fit my specific use case? A real estate agent should ask about MLS-ready exports. A winery or hospitality client should ask about golden-hour scheduling. A couple booking wedding coverage should ask how the operator keeps the drone discreet and safe around guests.

Red Flags That Should Make You Look Elsewhere

  • Can't produce a Remote Pilot Certificate, or gets defensive when asked.
  • A portfolio that's entirely stock footage or demo-library clips rather than actual local work.
  • No written scope of deliverables, formats, or revision rounds before the shoot.
  • No real answer for how they'd handle a restricted or coastal flight location.
  • Vague or no policy on weather-related rescheduling.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it enough if someone just says they're "FAA licensed"?

Not on its own. Ask for the certificate directly and see if the operator can describe, in their own words, what Part 107 actually requires. An operator who holds the certificate will not hesitate to talk about it specifically.

Is a noticeably cheaper quote with no mention of licensing ever worth the risk?

Generally no. An unlicensed flight done for pay is not legal, and if something goes wrong, there's no insurance backing the job and no accountability if the footage is unusable.

How far in advance should I book an aerial shoot in San Luis Obispo County?

A few weeks of lead time gives the most flexibility around weather and lighting, particularly for projects where golden-hour timing or a clear coastal morning matters to the result.

Can one shoot cover real estate, social, and brand needs at once?

Often yes, if you scope that upfront. Combining uses in a single flight is usually more efficient than booking separate shoots for each channel.
